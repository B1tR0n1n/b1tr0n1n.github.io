The OS Has No Concept of a Machine Actor

On delegation, authority, and the layer we forgot to build.

b1tr0n1n — September 2026
OVERLORD on GitHub →

The user account was invented for a person. When you log in, the operating system is not tracking a body or a mind — it is tracking a set of authorities. What you may read, what you may write, what you may kill. Behind those authorities sits an assumption so old nobody states it anymore: that on the other side of the account is a human being with intent, memory, and something to lose. You can be fired. You can be sued. You can be asked, afterward, what were you thinking, and be expected to answer.

We have now handed that account to something that is none of those things. An agent logs in as you, inherits every authority you hold, and acts at machine speed across your filesystem, your network, and your credentials. The OS sees your user id and asks no further questions. It cannot, because it has no vocabulary for the thing that is actually operating. It knows about users. It has never had a concept of a machine actor.

The account models a person. The agent is not a person. It inherits everything a person has, and owes nothing a person owes.
· · ·

What the Account Actually Models

Strip a user account down and it is a bundle of three things: an identity, a set of permissions attached to that identity, and an implicit contract that a responsible party stands behind both. Unix got this right for the world it was built for. A person is durable. A person's intent persists across a session. A person can be held to account for what happened under their id, which is why permissions are attached to identity in the first place — the identity is the thing you can punish.

An agent breaks all three assumptions at once. Its identity is borrowed, not its own. Its intent lives in a prompt that the OS never sees and could not interpret if it did. And there is no one to hold to account at the resolution where the damage happens — not the model, which has no continuity, and not quite the human, who typed a sentence and got a thousand syscalls. The chain from instruction to consequence runs straight through a layer the operating system is blind to.

So we do the only thing the current model allows. We give the agent a person's authority and hope. Every harness vendor then bolts on its own guardrails — a permission prompt here, an allowlist there — reinventing, badly and in isolation, a boundary the OS should have owned. This is not a failure of any one product. It is a missing primitive.

· · ·

Four Things the OS Should Enforce

I spent thirteen months thinking about what that primitive should be, and then a week building it, and the shape that fell out has four parts. None of them are novel in isolation. The point is that they belong together, underneath the harness, in the layer that touches the kernel.

Capability, not identity. An agent should not receive an account. It should receive a grant — these paths, this much network, this long, and no more. In the Marine Corps we called the compressed version of this commander's intent: the constraints and the objective, stated tightly enough that a subordinate under fire, out of contact, could act correctly without asking. A capability is commander's intent expressed as something the kernel enforces rather than something the agent is trusted to remember.

Provenance. Every mutation should trace back to the actor, the instruction, and the reasoning that produced it. Not a log you consult after the fire — a flight recorder that runs whether or not anything goes wrong, so that the question what were you thinking finally has an answer even when the thing that was thinking has no memory of its own.

Reversibility. This is the keystone. Delegation stalls on a single fear: what if it breaks something I can't undo. Remove the fear and the whole calculus changes. Agent action should be transactional — snapshot, execute, inspect, then commit or rollback. The agent runs against a fully writable world and exits believing everything happened. Nothing is real until a human, or a policy, looks at the diff and says so.

Arbitration. One agent is a permissions problem. Ten agents contending for the same resource is a scheduling problem, and the OS already knows how to schedule contention — it does it for the CPU every microsecond. Authority should be arbitrated the same way. Nobody needs this yet. Everybody will.

The Industry Arrives at the Same Door

When I started, the honest pitch was "nobody is doing this." That is no longer true, and the fact that it stopped being true is the strongest evidence I have that the gap was real. In the middle of 2026 Microsoft shipped a Windows Agent Runtime with per-agent capability grants scoped to exactly three axes — filesystem, network, application launch. Researchers began publishing OS-level policy layers for agent harnesses and millisecond-granularity rollback sandboxes. Every one of them had, independently, walked up to the same door I had.

That is not discouraging. It is the market confirming the thesis on my behalf. The interesting question is no longer whether the machine actor needs its own primitive — the platforms have conceded that — but who owns the layer and what it refuses to compromise on. A platform vendor will build this into their platform, and you will run your agents inside their world, on their terms. The alternative is a boring, load-bearing primitive that belongs to no one: model-agnostic, running on the Linux box the agent is already on, that you can read in an afternoon. The SQLite pattern, not the Windows pattern.

· · ·

The Part Still Unsolved

I would rather tell you where the frontier is than pretend I stand past it. Two edges are still sharp.

The first is the merge back. Most sandboxes isolate an agent into a throwaway environment and then discard it, or snapshot a frozen world and restore it. That is the easy version. The hard version is committing an agent's work back onto a live tree that other processes have been changing the whole time — re-verifying every file's fingerprint, refusing to silently clobber a human's concurrent edit, three-way merging the changes that don't collide. Reversibility only matters if you can eventually make it real without destroying what moved underneath it. That problem is where most of the difficulty actually lives, and it is not close to fully solved.

The second edge is harder still, and it is honest to name it: a filesystem snapshot cannot un-send a network call. Reversibility has a hard boundary at the moment an action leaves the machine. You can roll back the disk. You cannot roll back the email, the payment, the API write that already landed on someone else's server. Anyone who tells you they have made agent action fully reversible is lying or has not thought about the network. The right posture is to make the reversible part genuinely reversible and to be loud about where the boundary is.

· · ·

I carried a radio in Afghanistan. The whole job was relaying commander's intent across a link that degraded exactly when it mattered most, to people who had to act on it alone, under friction, with the plan already coming apart. You learn quickly that intent has to survive the gap between the one who holds it and the one who executes it — and that the link is where everything is won or lost.

We have opened a new gap. On one side, a human with intent and accountability. On the other, a machine actor that executes at a speed and scale no human can supervise in the loop. The operating system sits exactly on that link and, for now, relays nothing — it just passes the authority through and looks away. OVERLORD is my attempt to build the thing that should live there: the layer that carries intent across the gap as enforceable constraint, records what was done in whose name, and lets a human decide, with the full picture in front of them, whether any of it becomes real.

The tool is on GitHub, MIT-licensed, with a red-team suite that invites you to break it. Structure reveals truth. Go find where mine is wrong.
